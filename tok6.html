There is a difference between: Commonly successful topics (which often become common because they are accessible and rich in knowledge questions) Overused topics (which can still score well but require exceptional treatment to avoid becoming generic) Original topics (which are not automatically better, but often allow students to demonstrate personal engagement) One thing several experienced IB examiners emphasize is that students often mistake an unusual object or example for an original idea. A student can write a brilliant exhibition about a common object (a passport, a calculator, a vaccine card) if the analysis is insightful, while a bizarre object can still produce superficial commentary. What Often Impresses Examiners Not obscure topics. Rather: Personal Knowledge Encounters Examples: A failed TSA engineering project A yearbook publication mistake A family photograph with disputed memories A medical diagnosis that later changed A local monument nobody notices A GPS error on a camping trip A guitar tuning system you use daily A classroom grading rubric A conversation that changed someone's belief These tend to feel authentic because they arise from the student's own knowledge journey. A Practical "TOK Blackout List" When students first brainstorm, I sometimes encourage them to temporarily ban: Climate change COVID Holocaust Galileo Einstein Social media AI Vaccines Smartphones Wikipedia for the first 30 minutes of ideation. The goal is not to prohibit them permanently. It is to force students to ask: "What knowledge questions exist in my own life before I reach for the examples everyone already knows?" Ironically, after that process, some students return to one of those classic examples—but now they understand why it matters rather than simply choosing the first example that came to mind.
