World Attention: Provenance, Method, and Bias Audit

Detailed technical and classroom methodology report | Daily Briefing v2

Executive finding. The intended module is a transparent daily attention map: 3–10 major clusters, where each is receiving attention, and the perceived source-orientation mix as percentages. The installed code is a strong prototype but currently prints only three clusters and displays orientation counts rather than percentages.

1. Purpose and epistemic boundary

World Attention was designed to answer three limited questions: What topics are receiving broad attention in the sample? What concrete object could students examine? What Theory of Knowledge question does that object invite? Its output is deliberately narrower than “the world’s most important news.” The six-edition English-language sample is a proxy rather than humanity.

The module observes distribution and recurrence. It does not open every article, compare claims with primary evidence, determine factual accuracy, judge moral importance, or measure public opinion. A high score means that a cluster appeared repeatedly across the retrieved editions and outlets under the scoring rules.

The master panel is the authoritative inspection surface; the briefing is a projection of it. Both layers must be populated from the same persisted refresh records so a student can trace every briefing claim to its underlying row without a second news fetch.

Headline integrity rule. A displayed headline is evidence and must remain verbatim and complete. Neither layer may shorten it with a substring limit, replace the ending with an ellipsis, or hide overflow. The linked source and outlet must remain attached to the same cluster record.

4. End-to-end propagation

  1. Network retrieval. Google News RSS edition feeds are requested during the full refresh.
  2. Parsing. RSS item titles and source labels are extracted and cleaned.
  3. Normalization. Headline tokens are lowercased, stripped of punctuation, deduplicated, and filtered through stopwords.
  4. Clustering. Similar headline signatures are grouped into candidate topics.
  5. Scoring and diversity selection. Clusters receive measurable attention scores; the current renderer selects up to three, while the target is a dynamic 3–10.
  6. Source-profile audit. Known family and orientation metadata are joined from a curated sheet; unknown remains unknown.
  7. TOK framing. Rule-based category, AOK, concept, object, “why,” and question fields are generated.
  8. Persistence. The complete record is written to DATA_WORLD_ATTENTION.
  9. Rendering. The print function reads the persisted rows and writes only the fixed World Attention block in PRINT_DAILY_TEST.

5. Retrieval layer: where the information comes from

The immediate upstream source is Google News RSS, not a hand-selected reading list. Six edition URLs are constructed with locale parameters for the United States, United Kingdom, Canada, Australia, India, and South Africa. The code requests up to 12 feed items per edition, for a theoretical maximum of 72 headline records per refresh. A failed edition is logged and skipped; the remaining editions can still produce a result.

6. Parsing and normalization

The parser extracts RSS <item> blocks, decodes XML entities, removes embedded markup, reads the <source> field when available, and otherwise attempts to infer a trailing outlet name from the headline. It removes the repeated “ - Outlet” suffix before analysis. Headlines with fewer than two usable tokens are discarded.

Tokenization lowercases the headline, removes apostrophes and punctuation, splits hyphenated forms, rejects purely numeric tokens, rejects tokens shorter than three characters, removes a defined stopword list, and keeps unique tokens. The stopword list includes ordinary function words plus editorial wrappers such as breaking, live, update, opinion, analysis, watch, video, and story.

Bias consequence. Token rules influence which headlines look similar. Names, transliterations, synonyms, euphemisms, and differently framed descriptions of the same event may fail to cluster. Conversely, headlines sharing a distinctive term may be grouped even when their underlying claims differ.

7. Clustering: how repeated stories are recognized

The system first calculates document frequency: how many retrieved articles contain each token. Each headline signature keeps up to eight relatively uncommon tokens, favoring rarer and longer terms. Very common tokens are excluded from the signature when they appear above the code’s frequency threshold.

Candidate articles are processed sequentially. An article joins an existing cluster when its signature shares at least two tokens with the cluster signature. One shared token can suffice only when it is at least six characters long and appears in no more than three retrieved articles. Otherwise a new cluster is created. Cluster signatures are recomputed as items are added.

The human-readable topic label is not generated from a free-form language model. The system selects a representative headline, removes common wrappers, and shortens it at a clause or word boundary. This reduces “token salad,” but it also means one outlet’s wording can become the visible label for a multi-outlet cluster.

8. Attention score and selection

Formula. Attention score = 12 × represented editions + 4 × distinct outlets + 3 × clustered articles + 8 if the topic overlaps the current trend list. The result is capped at 100.

The score rewards geographic-edition recurrence most heavily, then outlet variety, then article volume. A trend overlap is a modest bonus based on token overlap with up to ten current terms already present in the briefing’s trends sheet. Because editions and outlets are not weighted by audience size, reputation, ownership, or evidentiary quality, the number should be read only as a recurrence signal.

ConfidenceRuleMeaning within this system
HIGHAt least 4 editions and 4 outletsStrong recurrence in this sample; not verified truth.
MEDIUMAt least 2 editions and 2 outletsCross-edition recurrence exists.
LOWAnything below the medium thresholdSparse or incomplete cross-edition evidence.

Final selection first prefers clusters present in at least two editions when enough such clusters exist. It sorts by edition count, attention score, and article count. During selection, a cluster receives a penalty when a previously selected item has the same category or shares multiple topic tokens. This is a diversity preference, not a political balance quota, and a sufficiently dominant story can still win.

9. Source families and perceived orientation

The source audit is intentionally conservative. New outlet labels are automatically added to DATA_SOURCE_PROFILES as an explicit review queue with orientation UNKNOWN. Existing profiles are never overwritten by the automated refresh. Ownership family and orientation are read only from human-reviewed profile fields.

Allowed orientation labels are LEFT, LEAN_LEFT, CENTER, LEAN_RIGHT, RIGHT, and UNKNOWN. For compact display, lean-left is counted in L, lean-right in R, center in C, and all unrecognized or unreviewed sources in U. The visible code L0 C0 R0 U3 therefore means zero profiled left outlets, zero center, zero right, and three unknown—not “three neutral sources.”

Critical limit. A source-orientation percentage is a composition statistic, not a measured percentage of bias in an article. Orientation describes a reviewed source profile, not the accuracy of a headline or claim. Ownership family counts can reveal apparent duplication, but they do not establish editorial independence.

11. Data persistence and print propagation

The refresh clears and rewrites DATA_WORLD_ATTENTION. Each selected topic preserves retrieval date and timestamp, rank, topic, attention score, edition and outlet counts, article count, category, TOK fields, representative headline, source list, edition list, confidence, trend overlap, ownership-family count, profiled and unprofiled outlet counts, and orientation summary.

The print renderer does not fetch news. It reads up to three persisted rows and writes a fixed World Attention region in PRINT_DAILY_TEST. Each topic card shows the score, edition/article evidence, source mix, family/profile status, orientation mix, a rule-generated “why,” AOK/concept, and suggested object. A fourth card displays the strongest TOK Lens and the explicit warning: ATTENTION ≠ TRUTH • DIVERSITY ≠ VERIFICATION.

When retrieval or clustering is insufficient, the system does not silently invent a normal result. It writes or renders low-confidence fallback language such as “Cross-region attention unavailable,” “Awaiting cross-region signal,” and “Not enough cross-region evidence yet.”

12. Where bias can enter

  • Platform bias: Google News decides which publishers and headlines appear in each edition feed.
  • Language bias: all six editions are English-language; other linguistic publics are absent.
  • Geographic bias: six countries cannot stand for the world, and edition boundaries do not equal audience boundaries.
  • Publisher bias: the sample reflects outlets indexed and surfaced by Google News, not all journalism or primary evidence.
  • Headline bias: clustering uses headlines rather than full reporting, evidence, corrections, or context.
  • Algorithmic bias: stopwords, thresholds, token overlap, category keywords, and diversity penalties encode design choices.
  • Profile bias: source-family and orientation labels depend on human review quality, update frequency, and chosen profile sources.
  • Teacher/system framing bias: TOK categories, suggested objects, and questions privilege certain concepts and forms of evidence.
  • Timing bias: a refresh is a snapshot. Breaking events, time zones, and feed update rhythms affect visibility.
  • Attention bias: frequently repeated stories can crowd out locally important, slow-moving, marginalized, or underreported knowledge.

13. Student audit protocol

  1. Read the label literally. Treat the card as an attention signal, not a verdict.
  2. Inspect the denominator. How many of six editions and how many distinct outlets produced the score?
  3. Read percentages with coverage. What share is L/C/R/U, and what percentage of outlets were actually profiled?
  4. Interrogate UNKNOWN. Unknown orientation means unprofiled, not unbiased.
  5. Open the underlying object. Move from the headline to the map, ruling, dataset, statement, study, or other primary artifact.
  6. Compare framing. How do different outlets name the actors, causes, evidence, uncertainty, and consequences?
  7. Search for absence. Which regions, languages, communities, and plausible stories are missing?
  8. Separate three judgments. Is it receiving attention? Is the claim verified? Is it important? These require different evidence.
  9. Audit the teacher. Students should challenge the chosen AOK, concept, object, and question—including the teacher’s interpretation.

15. Recommended classroom disclosure

Disclosure. World Attention is an automated snapshot of 3–10 major repeated headline clusters across sampled news editions. It shows where attention appears and the perceived orientation mix of identified outlets. These percentages describe source composition—not factual accuracy or the amount of bias in an article. Students are expected to challenge the sample, profiles, classifications, omissions, and the teacher’s interpretation.
